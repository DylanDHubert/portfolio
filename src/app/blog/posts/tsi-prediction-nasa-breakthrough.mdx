# Predicting the Sun's Energy Output from Images of the Sun

This was my first machine learning project, and still my favorite. As an undergraduate researcher in a university lab collaborating with NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, I worked on forecasting **Total Solar Irradiance (TSI)**: the total solar power reaching Earth, measured in watts per square meter. My bet was that the answer was sitting in plain sight, in pictures of the Sun that the model wasn't being shown.

## The Problem

TSI sits a little above 1360 W/m², but it is not constant. It wobbles by roughly 0.1%, and those wobbles are what matter. We modeled the **TSI perturbation** (`TSI − 1362 W/m²`) rather than the absolute value, which gives the model a signal centered on the variation instead of on a huge constant offset.

<img src="/images/nasa/TSI_graph.png" alt="TSI perturbation (TSI minus 1362 W/m²) from 2011 to 2013, with sharp dips such as the one in August 2011" width="100%" />

Look at the sharp downward spikes, like the one in August 2011. Those dips happen when large **sunspot groups** rotate across the face of the Sun. Sunspots are cooler, darker patches of intense magnetic field, and when big ones face Earth, less light reaches us. Bright magnetic regions called **faculae** push the other way.

### Why it matters

TSI is the main energy input to Earth's climate system, so accurate records and forecasts feed directly into energy-budget and climate research.

It also connects to space weather. The magnetically complex **active regions** that cause TSI dips are the same regions that produce **solar flares and coronal mass ejections (CMEs)**, the events that disrupt satellites, radio communication, and power grids. A model that learns to find and weigh active regions from solar imagery is learning to read the same features that space weather forecasting depends on.

## Where the Lab Was

The lab had already done extensive work. Their best baseline was an **Informer** (a transformer built for long time series) fed with **Singular Spectrum Analysis (SSA)**. SSA breaks a signal into many sub-signals: slow trends, periodic cycles, and noise. The baseline used a window size of 1K and then recombined those sub-signals into just three: main, trend, and noise.

I spent my first semester with Dr. Ding re-running the baseline to learn the data, and learning PyTorch from scratch along the way: data splits, learning rates, layer design, debugging gradient flow, all before LLMs could help. Once the data made sense, I had two ideas.

## Idea 1: Stop Throwing Away the SSA Signals

Recombining the SSA output into three components is a human decision about which frequencies belong together. I wanted to know whether the model could make that decision better.

So I passed the **individual SSA sub-signals** straight to the model and let it learn their weights. It worked better than the manual recombination. I also found that a window of 10 versus 1K made very little difference, which made the full decomposition cheap to use.

## Idea 2: Let the Model See the Sun

The baseline only saw numbers summarizing the Sun. But the cause of the biggest TSI swings is visible in the images from the **Helioseismic and Magnetic Imager (HMI)** on NASA's Solar Dynamics Observatory. The team knew the images could help; I pushed to use them and worked out how.

### Preprocessing: Finding the Active Regions

An HMI image of the Sun is mostly a granulated orange disk. That texture is convection, the Sun's normal surface, and to a model it is mostly noise. The signal is in the small areas of **high information density**: sunspots and faculae.

I built the filter by hand with OpenCV, tuning each constant by eye. It does three things: a **high-pass filter** strips the smooth disk, so only fine structure remains. **Blurring and taking the magnitude** let random granulation cancel out while clustered sunspot groups survive. Then **two thresholds** keep only the strongest structure, the first absolute and the second relative to the brightest surviving pixel.

## The Architecture: CNN + Informer

The final model has two branches that meet in the middle: a CNN that reads **space** (what the Sun looks like) and SSA features that carry **time** (how the signal has been moving). Tap any block to see what it does.

<SolarArchitecture />

A few details:

- **The CNN is deliberately simple.** Repeated `Conv3D → ReLU → MaxPool → BatchNorm` blocks grow the channels 1 → 32 → 64 → 128 → 1024, then global pooling turns each frame into a 1024-number feature vector. No residuals or attention: its only job is to turn images into features.
- **Fusion is concatenation.** 1024 image features + 10 SSA sub-signals + 6 statistical features (mean, median, mode, and others) give a 1040-dimensional vector per timestep.
- **A small MLP** projects that vector down to the Informer's `d_model`.
- **The Informer** is set by four numbers: `d_model` (feature size per timestep), `seq_len` (how much history it sees), `label_len` (how many known target values it gets as a starting point), and `pred_len` (how far ahead it predicts, all at once rather than one step at a time).

## How We Tested It

We evaluated the model in three settings, from easiest to most useful. Switch between them below to see what the model gets to see and what it has to predict.

<SolarScenarios />

- **Single-point fill:** predict one missing value with data on both sides. Mostly a sanity check.
- **Gap fill:** predict a run of hundreds or thousands of missing points with data on both sides, which is useful for repairing instrument outages.
- **Forecasting:** predict forward with no future data at all. This is the hard one and the one that matters.

## Results

Single-point fill reached near-perfect accuracy early. Gap filling and forecasting improved steadily rather than dramatically, measured by mean squared error.

That is normal for real research. Fifty-percent improvements are usually a sign of a leaky test set, not a breakthrough. What mattered was that the approach **beat the mathematical models the solar physicist was using**, which is why the collaboration kept investing in it.

## From Lab to NASA Internship

After that semester I interned with Dr. Wu, the solar physicist on the NASA side. The goal was to extend the model beyond HMI to more **SDO channels at different wavelengths**, which show the Sun's atmosphere at different heights.

We built the multi-channel CNN, but the dataset grew to terabytes, and we couldn't secure the compute to test it properly. It was an early lesson that infrastructure often limits research as much as ideas do.

## What I Learned

1. **Ask what information you are throwing away.** Both of my contributions came from that one question: the discarded SSA sub-signals and the unused images.
2. **Domain knowledge drives preprocessing.** Knowing that granulation is normal and sunspots are signal mattered as much as the network design.
3. **Simple, hybrid architectures work.** A plain CNN feeding a transformer was enough to combine spatial and temporal information.
4. **Test more than one way.** The three evaluation settings showed very different sides of the model.

After this project I moved on to cloud modeling work, but this question still shapes how I approach ML problems.
